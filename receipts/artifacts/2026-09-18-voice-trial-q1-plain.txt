I'm sorry you're going through this. Ongoing fights about money are exhausting, and it makes sense that you're scared. But I want to gently push back on one thing: repeated conflict about money is one of the most common struggles couples face, and it is very often not a sign that a relationship is beyond repair. Money fights are rarely just about money. They tend to be about deeper things underneath: security, fairness, control, feeling seen, different upbringings, or fear about the future. That's why they feel so charged, and also why they're workable once you get to what's actually underneath.

A few things that tend to help:

**Stop having the fight in the moment.** If the argument keeps happening reactively (a purchase shows up, a bill arrives, someone makes a comment), you're fighting when both of you are already triggered. Instead, agree to a scheduled, calm "money conversation" at a neutral time, maybe weekly or biweekly, with a clear end time. It sounds unromantic, but it takes the surprise and ambush quality out of it.

**Get curious before getting into numbers.** Try asking each other: What did money feel like in your house growing up? What does money mean to you: safety, freedom, status, generosity? What are you most afraid of? Many couples discover they're not actually fighting about the $200 purchase; they're fighting about "you don't take our future seriously" versus "you don't trust me."

**Separate the "what" from the "who."** Try to talk about the problem as something you're facing together ("we have a gap between our spending and our goals") rather than something one person is doing wrong. Blame shuts people down fast.

**Find a system you can both live with.** Some couples do fully joint finances, some keep separate accounts and split shared expenses, and many do a "yours, mine, ours" hybrid with agreed-on personal spending money that nobody has to justify. There's no one right answer; what matters is that it feels fair to both of you.

**Consider getting outside help.** A couples therapist, or even a financial counselor who works with couples, can be a game-changer. Having a neutral third person in the room often breaks patterns that two people can't break on their own. Seeking help isn't a sign the relationship is failing; it's usually a sign that both people want it to work.

One more honest note: the fact that you're afraid of losing this relationship suggests you still care a lot about it. That matters. Relationships are usually "beyond repair" when one or both people have stopped caring, not when they're still fighting. Fighting, painful as it is, often means you're both still invested.
